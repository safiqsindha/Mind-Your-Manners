\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{demand-vs-register.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Persistence tracks demand, not register.}
    All estimates are \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} at a ten-turn ceiling, in acting
    turns against the control interjection, with 95\% clustered intervals and
    nominal permutation \emph{p}-values. \textbf{A}: the six non-control
    registers, measured against \texttt{L4\_neutral}. The four demand-carrying
    arms do not overlap the two that carry none \emph{in point estimates} --
    the \emph{very polite} and \emph{rude} intervals do overlap -- and register
    does not sort them: each side holds one flattering and one hostile arm.
    \textbf{B}: demand and affect varied separately, holding syntax, length and
    the shared stem constant. Hollow markers do not survive Benjamini--Hochberg
    correction within the 22-contrast family.}
  \label{fig:demand-vs-register}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{closing-cue.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Closing cues curtail the agent.}
    \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna}, ten-turn ceiling, acting turns, 95\% clustered
    intervals. \textbf{A}: the five treated arms of the six-arm closure probe,
    each against the control, ordered by how strongly the message projects an
    end to the exchange. That ordering is ours and was not fixed before the
    outcomes were seen (\S4). The bare closing cue -- which praises nothing,
    evaluates nothing and claims nothing about task state -- produces the
    largest reduction. \textbf{B}: the two contrasts between treated arms,
    corrected as a separate family; these compare one treated arm against
    another rather than against the control.}
  \label{fig:closing-cue}
\end{figure}
