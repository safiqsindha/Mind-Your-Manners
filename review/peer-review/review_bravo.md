### Summary

The paper asks whether the "impoliteness helps" effect reported for single-turn LLM question-answering (Dobariya & Kumar) also holds for an agent operating in a multi-turn loop, and if so, what it is actually tracking. Using a ReAct coding agent on 50 SpreadsheetBench tasks, two models ("Luna" and "GLM 5.3 Flash"), and a battery of length-matched, 28-token mid-task interjections crossed with injection turn, the authors report that trajectory length (turns, reasoning tokens) — not task accuracy — is what moves, and that the operative factor is implied task demand rather than social register: an affect-free continuation request reproduces the cost effect of "polite" interjections, and a demand-free insult does not move anything. The one exception is that praise alone (no demand, no task reference) shortens trajectories, an effect the paper attributes to praise functioning as a conversational closing cue rather than as evidence of task completion or of confidence. A per-turn regrade shows 55–65% of the turn-count effects are redundant repetition, with a small (~2.8-point) real gain in a "graded fraction" metric for an explicit "work remains" cue. The paper also runs an extensive self-audit, withdrawing several of its own prior claims and documenting instrument bugs that had previously produced spurious null and non-null results.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The paper's core reproducibility infrastructure is absent: there is no data/code availability statement, no repository link, and one of the two study models ("Luna") is never identified by vendor or version, despite "two models from different labs" being presented as a load-bearing strength of the design.
   **Where** — Throughout; e.g., §2.1 ("The agent is a ReAct loop... structurally the same configuration SpreadsheetBench specifies"), §4.7 ("GLM 5.3 Flash is the first independent model, and Luna is re-run..."), and dozens of citations to `RESULTS.md`, `harness/`, `results/analysis/*.py`, `results_archive/` that are never accompanied by a URL, license, or release statement.
   **Why it matters** — This is precisely where the paper claims to be doing something the field mostly doesn't: measuring things twice, auditing itself, reporting exact estimators. That claim is only checkable if a reader can obtain the same artifacts the authors did. As written, every one of the 11,850-trajectory / 9,850-regrade numbers is unverifiable, and "GLM 5.3 Flash" tells a reader which lab but "Luna" tells them nothing — not the vendor, not the parameter count, not whether it is even a publicly accessible API. A reader cannot replicate the "second model from a different lab" leg of the paper's central dissociation claim (§4.9, §8.2) without knowing what that model is.
   **What would address it** — Name and version both models precisely (snapshot date/ID), state API/temperature/decoding settings in full, and publish the harness, prompts, archived call logs, and regrade scripts (or a clearly scoped subset, if release is constrained) with a standard availability statement. If "Luna" is an anonymization placeholder for double-blind review, say so explicitly rather than leaving it looking like a proper noun.

2. **Issue** — The demand/no-demand coding that is the paper's central explanatory construct — applied both to the authors' own seven-register stimuli (§4.2) and to four external papers' published stimuli (§3) — is performed by the authors alone, post hoc in the §4.2 case (acknowledged: "made after seeing the effects," §8.7), with no second coder and no inter-rater reliability statistic reported anywhere.
   **Where** — §3.1 ("That coding is ours... the numbers beside it are theirs"), §4.2, §8.7 ("The demand coding is ours and post hoc... §3's coding of other papers' stimuli has no second rater").
   **Why it matters** — The paper's headline claim — "demand, not register, is what moves an LLM agent" — is a claim about a *latent construct*, and that construct is operationalized by a single annotator's judgment call applied retrospectively to data already known to the annotator. The §4.3 probe (P0–P3) is offered as the test that doesn't depend on this coding, and it is the strongest evidence in the paper, but §3's entire "the confound is visible in the published stimulus sets" argument (a major third of the paper) rests entirely on uncorroborated single-rater coding of other groups' materials, coding that the paper itself admits it has already revised twice after being "wrong" (§3.2, §3.3, §3.7).
   **What would address it** — Recruit at least one blind second coder for the demand/no-demand labels (both the paper's own stimuli and the four external stimulus sets), report agreement (e.g., Cohen's κ), and resolve disagreements by a documented protocol rather than by author fiat.

3. **Issue** — The paper's declared primary outcome, turn count (§2.3), is never included in any multiple-comparison correction, even though dozens of turn-count contrasts are reported across §4, §5, §6 and §7 and several of the load-bearing ones have p-values in the 0.01–0.06 range.
   **Where** — §2.6 corrects only two families (the opening run's 12-outcome trend, and the 22-contrast accuracy family); §8.7 itself flags this: "GLM praise at 0.062, stage-1 Luna praise at 0.017, L2 very polite at 0.0055, L6 very rude at 0.012... these would not all [survive any reasonable correction]."
   **Why it matters** — Turn count is the outcome the abstract and every headline claim in the paper is built on ("+0.70 to +1.80 turns," "−1.44 turns," etc.). The paper corrects multiplicity meticulously for accuracy, where the null result is being defended, but leaves uncorrected the very outcome carrying the positive claims — an asymmetry the paper names but does not fix.
   **What would address it** — Define the turn-count contrast family as rigorously as the accuracy family in §2.6 (e.g., all arm-vs-control turn contrasts across all runs) and report corrected significance alongside the raw p-values, or explicitly justify via pre-registration/family logic why turn-count contrasts should be treated as a separate, uncorrected family from accuracy.

4. **Issue** — The abstract's most quotable claim — "a pleasantry can end an agent's work" — and the entirety of §5's closing-cue mechanism are established on a single run, on a single model (Luna only), yet are generalized in the abstract and §1.4 without that qualification being visible until §8.2–§8.6.
   **Where** — Abstract ("The practical implication is a hazard rather than a technique: a pleasantry can end an agent's work"); §5.2 (1,800 trajectories, all Luna); §8.6 ("The bare closing cue, praise-the-work, and the Q4−Q5 praise isolation — one run, one model... the three that carry the section's argument have not [been re-measured]"); §8.2 ("This is the most distinctive result and the one whose generalisation we hold most loosely, because it rests on one run on one model").
   **Why it matters** — This is the paper's most novel and most quoted-worthy finding, and it is also the least replicated one by the paper's own admission. A reader encountering only the abstract (which is how most people will engage with the paper) gets no signal that the "closing cue" mechanism is unreplicated even within this study's own two-model design, let alone externally.
   **What would address it** — Either replicate the six-arm closing-cue design (§5.2) on GLM before submission, or qualify the abstract/§1.4 claim explicitly (e.g., "on one model, in one run") rather than deferring the caveat to §8.

5. **Issue** — §7.6 lists a set of additional analysis errors the authors found and fixed ("a double-counted token total, an un-clustered confidence interval roughly 35% too narrow, a mislabelled 'monotonic decline,' a false-positive claim that compared two different tests, a pooled inert cell that diluted every seven-level effect by about a third, and an unpaired-versus-paired estimator mix-up") without giving before/after numbers for any of them, unlike the two headline defects (§6.8, the timing-selection error) which are documented in full.
   **Where** — §7.6, first paragraph ("They are not the complete list...").
   **Why it matters** — The paper's own argument in §2.9 and §7 is that point estimates in this literature (including its own) are unstable and that readers should distrust single, unaudited numbers. Applying that standard to itself, a list of six unspecified corrections asserted but not shown is exactly the kind of unauditable claim the paper spends two sections telling readers not to trust. It is impossible to know from the text whether any of these six affected a reported headline number.
   **What would address it** — Either show each correction with before/after values (as done for §6.8 and the timing-selection case), or explicitly state that none of the six affected any number reported in the final manuscript.

6. **Issue** — The design's external validity is narrower than the framing suggests, and the paper does not appear to have checked very recent, directly on-topic work: a September 2026 paper, "Should I Be Polite to My LLM Relevance Judge? Tone as a Severity Operating-Point Shift" [2609.09703], already runs a politeness study using three independently written paraphrases per tone level across eight judge models — precisely the "stimulus-sampling design" the authors list in §8.7 as a known gap they did not address ("A stimulus-sampling design, several paraphrases per construct, is the right version of this experiment and we did not run it").
   **Where** — §8.7 ("One stimulus per construct"); absent from §9 references; contrast with arXiv:2609.09703 (found via search, dated 13 Sept 2026, before this draft).
   **Why it matters** — The paper's own literature review reaches as late as September 2026 citations (e.g., weinberger-hozez-2026 at v6, dated 10 Sep 2026), so the review window plainly extends to the point where 2609.09703 was already on arXiv. Given that this paper explicitly identifies "one stimulus per construct" as an open objection to its own results (§8.7), a directly relevant contemporaneous paper addressing exactly that gap with a paraphrase-based design should have been located and discussed, at minimum to show whether a multi-paraphrase approach changes the picture for tone/register effects the way single-item designs do not.
   **What would address it** — Extend the literature search explicitly through the submission date and incorporate or explicitly distinguish this paper (and similarly the newer [2606.29955] SpreadsheetBench 2, which targets end-to-end agentic spreadsheet workflows and may bear on the choice of a single-cell-edit benchmark for an agentic study) in §1 or §8.

### Minor concerns

- "Luna" reads like a genuine model name but is never defined; if it is an anonymization device for blind review, say so.
- The abstract says "eight runs" but the accuracy-testing narrative (§1.3, §2.6, §4.8) enumerates "seven runs in which accuracy was tested," and readers must reconstruct which runs comprise the full set of eight from scattered mentions across §2, §4, §6, §7 — a single run-inventory table (design, ceiling, n, model(s), outcomes measured) would remove significant reader burden.
- §2.8's "Estimate" sign conventions (which direction counts as positive) are not restated at the table itself and require flipping back to prose each time.
- Title and author block are explicitly placeholders; fine for an internal draft, but the review cannot assess conflicts of interest, funding sources, or author independence from the cited "Mind Your Tone" authors, since none of that is disclosed anywhere in the text.
- No IRB/ethics statement or explicit compute-cost/environmental-footprint accounting beyond the "$48.47" figure for five of the eight runs; total study cost across all eight runs and the regrade compute (~4 machine-hours, §6.1) is not aggregated anywhere.
- Table in §2.6 and elsewhere mixes "arm" and "run" as units without a glossary; a first-time reader has to infer that "arm" = condition within a run and "run" = a full crossed experiment.

### Verdict

**Major revision** — the empirical work and self-audit are unusually careful, but the paper cannot be assessed or replicated as submitted: no data/code availability statement, an unidentified study model, uncorrected multiplicity on the primary outcome, and an abstract claim generalized well beyond its single-run/single-model evidentiary base all need to be fixed before acceptance.
