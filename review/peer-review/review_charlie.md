### Summary

The paper argues, via a large factorial study on a coding agent (SpreadsheetBench, two models, 50 tasks, ~11.8k trajectories), that a mid-task interjection's effect on agent turn count is driven by implied task *demand* rather than social register — with the single asymmetric exception that praise (and a content-free "closing cue") independently *shortens* trajectories, which the authors interpret through conversational pre-closing structure rather than completion or confidence inference. A per-turn regrade attributes most of the turn-count effect to redundant, non-improving steps, and the paper finds no accuracy effect that survives multiplicity correction or replication. The self-audit (§7) is genuinely unusual for this literature: six withdrawn claims, an instrument bug found and fixed, ranges reported instead of point estimates. My overall stance: this is one of the more methodologically self-aware papers of its kind, and it should be taken seriously — but the entire causal edifice rests on a "control" interjection whose own zero-effect status versus *no interjection at all* is validated exactly once, underpowered, on one model. Everything else in the paper is a contrast against that unvalidated zero point.

### Major concerns

1. **The cross-model "replication" of the praise leg is not actually significant** — `[Statistical rigour]`
   - **Where:** §4.7, table and prose ("GLM's praise arm does not reach significance on its own... the replication rests on the demand arm").
   - **Why it matters:** §4.7 is titled "It replicates on a second model," which a skimming reader will take as confirmation of the full three-way dissociation (demand up, praise down, insult flat) on GLM. In fact only the demand leg clears significance on GLM (praise p = 0.062, insult n.s. anyway — but insult being n.s. is the *predicted* null, so that's not informative either way). The paper's most novel and distinctive finding — that positive register alone reverses direction — has been shown on one model only. Section 8.3 admits this ("the replication of §4.7 rests on the demand arm") but the section title and abstract framing ("Manners are not the operative variable... reproduces across... two models from different labs") don't carry that qualifier forward consistently.
   - **What would address it:** Either retitle §4.7 to state precisely which legs replicated, or add trials/power to GLM's praise arm until it clears its own MDE.

2. **The demand/register coding that organizes the entire paper is applied post hoc, and the one prospective test of it (§4.3) still uses hand-written stimuli built to match the coding** — `[Evidence — post-hoc-shaped hypothesis]`
   - **Where:** §4.2 (explicit: "made after seeing the effects"), §8.7.
   - **Why it matters:** The paper is commendably honest that the seven-register demand coding in §4.2 was assigned after seeing the turn-count numbers, and that the correlation-based version of that argument (r = +0.85) is "close to curve-fitting." The rescue is §4.3's four-arm probe (P0–P3), which is prospective in the sense that the four texts were authored to instantiate the coding scheme, then tested once. But a single author-written sentence per cell is still one degree of freedom short of an independent test of the *construct* "demand" versus a test of "the specific sentence we picked to represent demand." Nothing rules out that P1's particular phrasing ("make sure that your final result is correct") is doing the work rather than task-demand-as-such.
   - **What would address it:** A second, independently-drafted set of demand/no-demand stimuli (ideally by a rater blind to the hypothesis), or at minimum several paraphrases per cell as in [2609.09703].

3. **No code, data, or model-identity release is stated, and one of the two models is an unidentifiable codename** — `[Reproducibility]`
   - **Where:** Throughout (citations to `RESULTS.md`, `harness/`, `results_archive/` as internal paths); model "Luna" used in every run without a vendor/version string; "GLM 5.3 Flash" is named but Luna never is.
   - **Why it matters:** A reader cannot tell which lab or model family "Luna" refers to, so "two models from different labs" is unverifiable as stated, and a competent grad student could not replicate the headline numbers without out-of-band information about what Luna actually is, at what API version, on what date (relevant given the drift concern the paper itself raises in §8.7). There's also no OSF/GitHub/Zenodo link anywhere in the draft, only internal script paths, which is a stronger reproducibility gap than the paper's own extensive engineering-failure post-mortems (§6.8, §7.7) would suggest the authors are otherwise inclined to accept.
   - **What would address it:** Name the model (or state explicitly why it's withheld, e.g. embargo/anonymization for review), release the harness and archived trajectory logs, and register the API-call dates against provider version pins.

4. **No dose-response test of "demand" — it's binary, from one wording, so "demand" is asserted to be a scalar causal lever without ever manipulating its strength** — `[Missing ablations]`
   - **Where:** §4.2–§4.3, §8.7 ("Every construct in this paper... is a single 28-token sentence").
   - **Why it matters:** The paper's central causal claim is that turn-count cost tracks *implied task demand*, but demand is operationalized as present/absent for one sentence, not as a graded quantity. Compare the seven-register run's own accidental confound (§2.2): wrapper length turned out to correlate with the effect better than the intended manipulation, which is exactly the failure mode a graded, multi-strength demand manipulation would catch early. Without a demand-intensity gradient, "demand" risks becoming a post-hoc label for "whichever sentences moved the needle," reintroducing concern #2 at the conceptual level.
   - **What would address it:** A three- or four-level demand-strength ladder (implicit request → explicit continue → explicit verify-and-persist → explicit deadline/stakes), crossed with the existing affect factor.

5. **The "pre-closing conversational structure" account is not distinguished from a cruder trained-pattern-matching account** — `[Evidence — alternative explanations]`
   - **Where:** §5.1–§5.4.
   - **Why it matters:** §5.1 invokes Schegloff & Sacks' conversation-analytic notion of pre-closing sequences as the vocabulary for why a bare "no further notes will follow" cue stops the agent harder than praise. But an equally parsimonious alternative — that RLHF/instruction-tuning has taught the model a shallow surface association between phrases like "final... no further... will follow" and wrap-up behavior, independent of any inferred discourse structure — is never posed as a competing hypothesis, only conversation-analytic pragmatics is. The paper does rule out *completion* and *confidence* readings carefully (§5.3), but "trained termination-keyword detector" would produce the same Q3/Q4/Q5 pattern the paper reports and is arguably the more parsimonious explanation for a model, as opposed to a human conversational participant.
   - **What would address it:** A stimulus that has the surface form of closure without the pragmatic content (e.g., a sentence about an unrelated topic that happens to contain "no further X will follow") versus one with the pragmatic content but atypical surface phrasing — to separate keyword-triggered pattern completion from genuine discourse-structural inference.

6. **The study's baseline — the "control" interjection every headline number is measured against — is validated as equivalent to "no interjection" exactly once, at low power, on one model, and never revisited when the ceiling, injection turn, or model changed** — `[Evidence — weak baselines]`
   - **Where:** §8.8: "Every crossed run compares an interjection against a *neutral interjection*, so the effects throughout are register effects, not interruption effects. Only the micro-experiment measured interruption itself — 3.47 to 4.11 turns under a neutral interruption, +51 reasoning tokens, p = 0.15 — once, on one model." Also §2.2's admission that the *first* control wrapper set was itself confounded by length before being fixed.
   - **Why it matters:** This is the load-bearing baseline of the entire paper. Every number reported — the +1.17 turns for "demand," the −1.44 for the closing cue, the accuracy equivalence bounds in §2.8 — is a contrast against a hand-written "routine status note" arm, not against a true no-interruption condition. The paper is careful to note this measures *register* effects rather than *interruption* effects, which is the right caveat for the within-study comparisons (since control is common across arms, differential effects should be unbiased by a constant interruption cost). But it does not address a subtler problem: if the "neutral" control text itself carries a small, unmeasured directional pull (e.g., because "this is a routine status note recorded" has faint demand-adjacent or closing-adjacent connotations — it is, after all, a message about documentation and record-keeping, delivered mid-task, structurally similar to the very Q0/P0 texts later re-used as controls in different sub-studies), then the reported magnitudes for every downstream arm are shifted by an unknown, un-estimated amount, and the sign of that shift is never bounded because the one check that exists is p = 0.15 on n small enough to miss anything short of a huge effect. A study whose central methodological contribution is "we measured things twice and reported ranges" (§7, §9's closing pitch to the field) never applies that same discipline to its own most consequential single quantity — the zero point everything else is measured from.
   - **What would address it:** Run the true no-interjection baseline at the same scale (crossed injection turns, both models, matched ceilings) as the main arms, not as a one-off underpowered micro-experiment, and report the control-vs-true-null contrast with the same rigor (paired/clustered estimator, bootstrap CI, TOST) applied everywhere else in the paper.

### Minor concerns

- The paper never lays out a single reconciled table of total trajectory counts across all sub-studies; a reader summing the individually reported ns (3,150 + 1,600 + 2,700 + 1,800 + …) cannot easily cross-check the abstract's "11,850 graded trajectories, eight runs."
- §2.8's table column header "TOST at ±4" gives bare numbers (e.g., 0.0014) without stating these are p-values for the equivalence test rather than effect sizes or CIs — a reader unfamiliar with TOST notation has to infer this from prose several paragraphs later.
- The draft is explicitly incomplete (placeholder title/author block, an "Outstanding before submission" checklist naming three unread references), which is fine for a preprint but means parts of §1 and §9 are provisional in ways a reviewer can't fully sign off on.
- "Luna" and "GLM 5.3 Flash" are inconsistently specified — GLM gets a version number, Luna does not, anywhere in the document.
- Several accuracy figures are reported in "points" without always restating "percentage points" on first use per section, which is a minor but repeated ambiguity given the paper's own insistence elsewhere on precise units.
- §3's stimulus-set audit of four prior papers is thorough to the point of self-indulgence relative to its own stated conclusion ("does not establish that every published effect... is an artefact of demand"); a reader has to work through several pages of table-by-table forensic detail to arrive at a fairly narrow claim.

### Belief update

Before this paper: my prior, shaped by the popular "rudeness improves accuracy" result and its own well-publicized non-replication, was that register effects on LLM outputs are probably real but small, noisy, and model-dependent, and that nothing was known about how register behaves in *agentic*, multi-turn settings specifically.

After this paper: I update toward believing that in at least one agentic coding-benchmark setting, an interjection's effect on how long an agent keeps working is driven substantially by whether it contains an explicit continue/verify instruction, and that praise-like closing language can independently shorten trajectories — this is a genuinely interesting and, as far as I can tell, unstudied phenomenon (mid-task register affecting agent persistence). I hold that update moderately rather than strongly, for three reasons tied to the concerns above: the cross-model check is incomplete for the paper's most novel finding (praise), the demand construct is operationalized from a single post-hoc-coded sentence, and — the concern I weight most — the zero point against which every number in the paper is computed is itself validated only by one underpowered, one-model check. My update on the accuracy question ("register doesn't move correctness by tone-literature-sized amounts") is smaller, since that is presented correctly as a replication of already-published single-turn findings rather than a new claim.

### Verdict

**Major revision** — the paper's central quantitative claims are all measured relative to a "neutral" control interjection whose equivalence to true silence is established by one p = 0.15 comparison on one model (§8.8), and until that baseline is validated at the same scale and rigor as the rest of the study, every headline turn-count and accuracy-equivalence number in the paper carries an unknown, unbounded offset.
