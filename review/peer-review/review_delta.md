# Review by delta

## Summary

The paper reports eight experimental runs (11,850 graded trajectories, two coding-agent models) probing whether the "polite prompts hurt / rude prompts help" effect reported in the single-turn MCQ literature (Dobariya & Kumar) also appears when a short interjection is dropped mid-task into an agentic coding loop on SpreadsheetBench. Its headline claims are: (1) turn-count cost is driven by an "implied task demand" component of an interjection, not by its politeness register, demonstrated by decomposing a seven-register cost curve into four demand-carrying and three non-demand arms with no overlap; (2) positive register alone (praise) is an exception that *shortens* trajectories, behaving like a conversational closing cue rather than a completion or confidence signal; (3) a per-turn regrade shows 55–65% of the turn-count effect is redundant work, with only a small (~2.8-point) real gain in graded-range correctness from an explicit "work remains" statement; (4) no accuracy effect survives multiplicity correction or replication across seven runs, framed as a bounded equivalence rather than a null; and (5) the authors' own point estimates are frequently unstable across re-measurement, including one reversal traced to a regrading-instrument bug. The paper is unusually self-auditing, cataloguing its own withdrawn claims and infrastructure failures in detail.

## Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The central "demand vs. register" dissociation is validated only by stimuli the authors themselves wrote to embody their own post-hoc coding scheme, with no independent manipulation check, no inter-rater reliability, and n=1 sentence per construct, so the "probe" in §4.3 risks being close to a demonstration that authors can write text that produces the effect they predicted rather than an independent experimental test of a pre-specified construct.
**Where** — §4.2 ("the coding of the seven registers was made after seeing the effects — we say so there"), §4.3 (the four hand-written 28-token probe sentences), §8.7 ("The demand coding is ours and post hoc... has no second rater... tests it on texts we wrote to embody our own coding").
**Why it matters** — If "demand" is operationalized as literally imperative continuation language ("please continue working... make sure your result is correct") and "register" as evaluative-adjective sentences with no instruction content, the finding that the former moves behavior and the latter does not is close to tautological: an agent that follows explicit instructions and ignores non-instructional commentary is unsurprising and does not require a "demand vs. manners" framing to explain. The paper's rhetorical claim to have refuted a politeness account rests on a construct whose boundary with "is or is not an instruction" is drawn by the same people who then wrote the test items and interpreted the results. Without independent stimulus validation (e.g., blind human or held-out-model ratings of "how much does this text demand continued work," collected before outcomes are known), the three-way dissociation in §4.9 cannot be distinguished from "explicit instructions change behavior, decorative text does not," which is a much weaker and less novel claim than the one advertised in the abstract.
**What would address it** — Pre-register (or at minimum construct-validate post hoc, with a held-out rating study or a second, blinded coder) a demand scale independent of the outcome data; generate multiple paraphrases per construct (the authors themselves flag "one stimulus per construct" as an open objection in §8.7 but do not act on it) and show the dissociation survives across paraphrases rated by an instrument that never saw turn-count outcomes.

2. **Issue** — The paper's "closing cue" construct (Q3: "this is the final note... no further notes will follow") is not cleanly separable from the "demand" construct it is contrasted against, because a statement that no more guidance is coming functions as an implicit instruction to finalize/wrap up, which is itself a form of task-relevant demand under the paper's own coding rule ("carries a demand if it instructs the model to do something about the task").
**Where** — §5.2–§5.4, where the bare closing cue produces the *largest* effect in the study (−1.443 turns) and is treated as evidence for a discourse-structure account distinct from task-content demand.
**Why it matters** — The paper's own coding rule in §3.1 defines demand broadly enough ("persist, verify, attend, be complete, or be brief") that "no further notes will follow" plausibly reads as an implicit brevity/completion instruction rather than a content-free discourse token. If so, the "register acts in one direction only" narrative (§4.4) and the "closing cue is content-free and outperforms register" narrative (§5.5) are in tension with each other: one treats absence of task content as diagnostic of a pure-register effect, the other treats the same absence as diagnostic of a pure-discourse effect, and the paper never rules out that Q3 is simply the strongest *implicit* demand in the set.
**What would address it** — An arm that removes the closing framing but keeps an equally strong implicit "you're on your own now" cue without the conversational "final note" language (e.g., varying only whether further monitoring is promised) would help separate discourse-closing pragmatics from an implicit stop-instruction reading.

3. **Issue** — The primary outcome was switched after seeing data, and several of the study's key population-restriction and estimator choices (control-defined comparison populations in §2.5, the paired-clustered vs. unpaired estimator choice in §2.4, the twenty-turn ceiling in §2.1) were adopted *after* earlier versions produced apparently anomalous or confounded results, which blurs the line between confirmatory and exploratory analysis throughout the paper.
**Where** — §2.3 ("Reasoning tokens were the pre-registered primary in the first run and were demoted on evidence"); §2.1 ("The turn ceiling is ours, and it changed mid-study... after the ten-turn ceiling was found to censor the continue-signal arms hardest"); §2.5 (comparison population fixed from control only after raw timing comparisons were found to be confounded by selection, §7.6).
**Why it matters** — Each individual fix is well-motivated and disclosed, and the paper deserves credit for transparency, but the cumulative pattern is that essentially every structural choice in the design (outcome variable, ceiling, comparison population, estimator) was revised in response to inspecting results from earlier runs on the same or overlapping tasks. This is a textbook garden-of-forking-paths setup even when each individual step is defensible, and it undermines the paper's own claim (§7.8) to be modeling good practice for a field it criticizes for underpowered, non-preregistered analysis. The paper never reports what a naive analyst using only the original pre-registered plan (reasoning tokens, 10-turn ceiling, unpaired estimator, raw timing contrasts) would have concluded, so a reader cannot assess how much of the final narrative is confirmatory versus reconstructed.
**What would address it** — A supplementary table showing the pre-registered analysis pipeline's results run through to completion, alongside the revised pipeline, so readers can see how much the substantive conclusions depend on the sequence of post hoc corrections.

4. **Issue** — There is no genuine no-interjection control in the main runs; every reported effect is relative to a "neutral interjection," and the only run that tested interruption-per-se against no interruption at all is a single, small, underpowered micro-experiment on one model.
**Where** — §8.8 ("The cost of being interrupted at all... Every crossed run compares an interjection against a neutral interjection... Only the micro-experiment measured interruption itself — 3.47 to 4.11 turns under a neutral interruption... once, on one model").
**Why it matters** — The entire "demand, not manners, moves the agent" claim is a claim about the *marginal* effect of content on top of a baseline interruption whose own cost relative to an uninterrupted trajectory is essentially unmeasured (n=1 underpowered run, p=0.15). If the neutral interjection itself already carries some demand-like pull (it is, after all, an execution-observation-embedded message the agent must process and could read as implicitly requesting continued attention), then all the reported turn deltas are differences between two doses of an unquantified base effect, not differences from a true zero-intervention baseline. This matters for external validity of the headline claim that "manners are not the lever": the paper cannot rule out that even its "control" condition already carries some demand relative to true silence.
**What would address it** — A properly powered no-interjection arm (or several) crossed with the existing design, so that the marginal cost of "any interjection" can be estimated with the same rigor as the register/demand contrasts.

5. **Issue** — The 50-task analytic sample is described only as "a strict subset of an earlier 100-instruction validation sample," with no stated selection rule, leaving open the possibility of selection on responsiveness to the manipulations under study.
**Where** — §2.1 ("Our sample is a fixed set of 50 instructions, a strict subset of an earlier 100-instruction validation sample... none of ours is [a no-op-passable task]").
**Why it matters** — The only disclosed selection criterion is exclusion of no-op-passable tasks; nothing rules out that the 50 were also filtered (even informally, across the "earlier 100-instruction validation sample" the authors ran) for tractability, non-degenerate variance, or other properties correlated with showing an effect. Given how many other choices in this study were revised after seeing intermediate results (concern 3), the provenance of the fixed 50-task sample is exactly the kind of decision that needs an explicit, dated selection rule to be credible as pre-treatment.
**What would address it** — State the exact rule used to go from 100 to 50 tasks, the date it was applied relative to any pilot analysis, and ideally show results are consistent on the excluded 50.

6. **Issue** — The interjection is delivered through the tool/execution-observation channel rather than as a user turn, which the paper acknowledges as an untested limitation (§8.5) but which undercuts the paper's framing of its results as a "replication" or extension of the single-turn tone literature into agentic settings.
**Where** — §1.2 ("it rides along with an execution observation rather than as a fresh user turn, and §8.5 records that as a limitation"); §8.5.
**Why it matters** — Because the tone literature it is positioned against manipulates genuine user-authored prompts, and because models are plausibly trained to weight user-channel content differently from tool-channel content, the paper's claim that "manners, not register, moves an LLM agent" (title) may not generalize to the most natural agentic analogue of a user being rude or polite mid-conversation — arguably the scenario practitioners actually care about. This is a design choice that directly limits how much the paper's conclusions transfer to real deployments, yet the title and abstract state the finding without this qualification.
**What would address it** — At minimum, one run delivering the interjection as a genuine user turn to check whether the demand/register dissociation and the praise-as-closing-cue result replicate through that channel.

## Minor concerns

- The paper does not cite or engage with arXiv:2512.12812 ("Does Tone Change the Answer? Evaluating Prompt Politeness Effects on Modern LLMs: GPT, Gemini, and LLaMA"), a directly relevant single-turn cross-model politeness study that postdates most of the cited tone literature and could bear on the generalization claims in §8.2.
- arXiv:2609.09703 ("Should I Be Polite to My LLM Relevance Judge?") reports model-dependent, sometimes U-shaped tone effects in an LLM-judge context and would strengthen or complicate the "affect and demand are entangled across paradigms" argument of §3; it is absent from the reference list.
- §2.6's multiplicity correction is applied to the accuracy family and the opening-run trend family, but the paper's own §8.7 concedes the primary turn-count outcome is uncorrected across many "load-bearing" contrasts (GLM praise p=0.062, Luna praise p=0.017, L2 p=0.0055, L6 p=0.012) — this deserves a formal correction or at least a clearly labeled secondary-family table rather than being raised only as an "objection we do not have an answer to."
- The claim that "Threatening... raises per-call reasoning tokens... sitting 20–30% above neutral" (§4.5) is described as "the exception," but no formal interaction test between arm and per-turn-token trajectory is reported — it reads as an eyeballed pattern.
- Table in §2.8 reports MDE and TOST columns without stating the exact TOST test statistic/procedure used beyond "bootstrap that resamples tasks jointly across runs," making independent recomputation difficult without the underlying scripts.
- §6.2's validation check found 7/126 (task 54513) systematically mis-scored by the regrade and calls it "undiagnosed" — for a section whose entire contribution is the trustworthiness of the regrading instrument (§6.8 documents a prior severe bug), an unresolved known failure mode this late in the pipeline is a yellow flag that should be investigated rather than parenthetically noted.
- Minor wording: the abstract states results are drawn from "eight runs," but Table/text in §4 and §5 reference at least nine distinct named runs (micro-experiment, seven-level, probe, praise run, stage 1, stage 1 GLM, ceiling-20 dedicated re-run, opening-tone run, plus the interrupted first attempt) — the run inventory would benefit from a single canonical table.

## Verdict

**Major revision** — the empirical scope, statistical self-scrutiny, and honesty about instability are genuine strengths, but the paper's headline dissociation rests on an under-validated, post-hoc, self-authored operationalization of "demand" versus "register" (concern 1) and lacks the basic construct-validity and no-intervention-baseline controls needed to support its strongest causal claims.
