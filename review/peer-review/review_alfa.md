LENS: Related work and novelty attribution
STANCE: Skeptical-but-fair

### Summary
The paper asks whether the "polite prompts hurt, rude prompts help" effect reported for single-turn LLM question-answering also describes what happens when a coding agent is interrupted mid-task by a register-varied message. Using a ReAct agent on 50 SpreadsheetBench tasks across two models (Luna, GLM), the authors deliver a single 28-token, length-matched interjection at controlled turns and measure turn count, reasoning tokens, and (via a custom per-turn regrade) progress toward the graded answer. The headline result is that turn-count effects are driven by implied task demand rather than politeness or rudeness per se — an affect-free "please continue" reproduces the cost, a length-matched insult with no demand does not move it — except that praise alone (no demand) *shortens* trajectories, an effect the authors attribute to praise functioning as a conversational closing cue rather than as a completion or confidence signal. A large fraction (55–65%) of the turn-count effect is shown, via a corrected per-turn regrade, to be redundant repetition; accuracy effects never survive correction or replication. The paper also devotes a full section (§3) to showing that the demand/register confound is visible in the published stimulus sets of four prior papers, and a full section (§7) to auditing its own instability, including one reversed conclusion traced to a regrading bug.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The paper's central related-work move — a systematic audit of "four papers from three groups" for a demand/register confound (§3) — is not actually a systematic literature search, and at least two directly on-topic tone-effect papers are absent from both that audit and the reference list.
   **Where** — §1.1, §3 (framed as covering the field), §9 References.
   **Why it matters** — The paper's novelty claim rests heavily on having characterized "this literature" exhaustively enough to say "the factor these papers vary is not the factor they name" (§1.4, §3.6). An arXiv search for "LLM agent politeness tone mid-task prompt trajectory length termination" surfaces at least two additional prompt-politeness studies not discussed: Cai et al., "Does Tone Change the Answer? Evaluating Prompt Politeness Effects on Modern LLMs: GPT, Gemini, and LLaMA" [2512.12812], posted December 2025 — well before the paper's own most recent citations (several dated mid-2026) — and Zhang & Li, "Should I Be Polite to My LLM Relevance Judge? Tone as a Severity Operating-Point Shift" [2609.09703]. The first is squarely inside the paper's own citation window and topic (multi-model tone-accuracy evaluation) and should either have been checked for the same demand/register entanglement or explicitly excluded with a stated reason; its absence is not flagged anywhere as a scoping choice, which makes "the tone literature" in §1.1 read as more complete than it is. This does not overturn the paper's within-study findings, but it weakens the specific claim that the confound has now been shown "in the published stimulus sets of the work this literature rests on" (§3.1) — that is a claim about the literature's completeness, and the literature reviewed is smaller than "this literature" suggests.
   **What would address it** — Either search and code [2512.12812] (and screen [2609.09703], noting it may postdate the review's freeze) for the same demand-vs-affect confound as §3.2–§3.5 do for the other four, or explicitly narrow the claim in §1.1/§3.6 from "this literature" to "the four most-cited/foundational papers we selected," with a stated selection criterion.

2. **Issue** — The novelty claim that mid-task, discourse-structural closing cues affecting agent termination are "as far as our literature review could establish... untested" (§5.1, §1.2) is asserted more strongly than the paper's own search discipline supports.
   **Where** — §1.2, §5.1, §5.5.
   **Why it matters** — A search for adjacent work turns up Munirathinam, "Will the Agent Recuse, and Will It Stop? Measuring LLM-Agent Compliance with In-Band Governance Signals at the Access Door and Mid-Flight" [2606.06460, v4 2026] — a paper specifically about in-band, mid-flight signals asking a *running* agent to stand down, which is conceptually adjacent to (though not identical to) the paper's closing-cue result. The paper's own honesty apparatus (§9's "Outstanding before submission" list, the explicit tagging of sources as "abstract only") shows the authors are aware that thoroughness claims need hedging, yet the phrase "as far as our review could establish, unoccupied" (§1.2) is not qualified the same way. Given the paper devotes an entire section (§3) to showing how confidently other authors have mischaracterized their own confounds, it is a comparable risk for this paper to assert a novelty gap without the same rigor it demands of others.
   **What would address it** — Either check [2606.06460] against the closing-cue claim and distinguish it explicitly (e.g., "governance/recusal signals are a different mechanism than discourse-closing pragmatics because X"), or soften "unoccupied" to something like "we found no prior work using this specific mechanism, though adjacent work on mid-flight stop signals exists."

3. **Issue** — The accuracy equivalence claim (§2.8, repeated in the abstract as "No accuracy effect survives correction or replication") rests on a pre-specified ±4-point bound that the design's own minimum detectable effect (3.7–7.8 points, median 5.52) barely clears, and TOST equivalence at that bound is reached in only 6 of 22 contrasts.
   **Where** — §2.7, §2.8, Table in §2.8, abstract.
   **Why it matters** — The abstract's compressed framing ("No accuracy effect survives correction or replication... we state that as an equivalence... rather than as an absence") is honest in the body text but risks being read by casual readers as a stronger null than the data support. Since MDE and the target effect size (4 points) are close together, most individual contrasts cannot actually rule out an effect the size the tone literature reports; only the *pooled* estimates can, and pooling introduces its own heterogeneity problems (Cochran's Q = 8.45, p = 0.038 for praise, resolved only by attributing it to a between-model random effect post hoc).
   **What would address it** — Nothing wrong needs fixing in the analysis itself (the paper is unusually careful here), but the abstract should make the "equivalence, not absence, and only after pooling with a bound close to the design's own detection limit" caveat more prominent, since it currently reads as a cleaner null than §2.7–§2.8 actually establish.

4. **Issue** — The paper's single most quotable practical claim — "a pleasantry can end an agent's work" — is built on a result (§5, the closing-cue analysis) that the paper itself classifies as measured once, on one model, at one turn ceiling (§8.6), yet it anchors the abstract and §1.4/§8.9's framing of the paper's contribution.
   **Where** — Abstract, §5.2–§5.4, §8.6.
   **Why it matters** — This is a claim-vs-evidence alignment problem compounded by the related-work gap above: because no prior work is cited that would let a reader calibrate how surprising or fragile this class of effect typically is (cf. concern 2), the headline claim is carrying more interpretive weight than a single-run, single-model result should. §7's own replication table (§7.2–§7.3) shows that of contrasts measured twice, the *smallest* effect (praise, ≈0.8 turns) was the one that was "marginally inconsistent" across measurements (z = 2.03, p = 0.042) — and the closing-cue arm, which produces the largest reduction in the whole study, was never re-measured at all.
   **What would address it** — Either re-run the `Q3_closing_neutral` contrast once more (the paper's own §7.8 recommends exactly this discipline for every load-bearing claim) before making it the headline, or downgrade the abstract's framing from a stated hazard to a flagged, single-measurement hypothesis, consistent with how §8.6 already (correctly) scopes it internally.

5. **Issue** — The demand/register coding scheme that underlies §3's confound argument and §4's dissociation is applied by the authors alone, post hoc in §4.2 (acknowledged) and without a second coder anywhere in the paper, including for the cross-paper stimulus coding in §3.
   **Where** — §3.1 ("That coding is ours"), §4.2, §8.7 ("The demand coding is ours and post hoc").
   **Why it matters** — The paper is unusually forthright about this ("§4.2's coding of the seven registers was made after seeing the effects... §3's coding of other papers' stimuli has no second rater," §8.7), which is commendable, but it remains a live threat to both the within-study dissociation claim and the cross-paper confound argument, since "carries a demand" is a judgment call (e.g., is "try to focus" a demand or an insult-plus-bare-request, §3.3) that could shift the reported splits (e.g., the "4 of 4 models" separation in §3.2 depends on classifying "Threatening" as non-length-constraining, which the authors themselves flag as the one debatable call).
   **What would address it** — A blinded second coder (even applied retrospectively to the already-published stimulus tables) with an inter-rater agreement statistic would substantially strengthen both §3 and §4's central claims, which currently rest on a single analyst's taxonomy applied after seeing the outcomes it is meant to explain.

### Minor concerns

- The manuscript is explicitly a draft (placeholder title/author block, "Draft status" boxes throughout, an open "Outstanding before submission" checklist in §9) — not obviously reviewable as a finished submission, though the empirical content is substantive enough to review as-is.
- The paper cites its own analysis scripts by path (`results/analysis/accuracy_null_mde.py`, etc.) throughout the text; these are not included or linked, so reproducibility claims cannot be checked by a reader without access to the associated repository, which is never named or linked.
- §2.9/§7 use "range" reporting as a stated best practice but the paper still leads with point estimates in the abstract (e.g., "−1.44 turns," "1.35 turns") without ranges, which is in some tension with its own stated recommendation ("We report ranges by default").
- The citation-verification apparatus in §9 (per-entry "Verified: full text / abstract only" tags, a running "Outstanding" list) is unusual for a research paper and reads more like an internal audit log than a references section; useful for transparency but it substantially inflates length and may not survive normal journal formatting.
- Several statistics are reported with more precision than their uncertainty would seem to justify given n = 50 tasks (e.g., "94.4%," "80.8%"); not incorrect, but the paper elsewhere argues strenuously against over-precise point estimates (§2.9, §7), so the inconsistency is a little jarring.
- The term "social register" is used throughout as if self-evidently defined, but the operational definition is scattered (borrowed from [kumar-dobariya-2026] per §9) rather than stated once in the introduction.

### Verdict
**Major revision** — the empirical core (the demand/register dissociation and the self-directed replication audit in §7) is careful and unusually transparent about its own limitations, but the paper's central novelty and related-work claims are not as exhaustively supported as the text asserts, at least one headline result (the closing-cue effect) is over-weighted relative to its single-measurement status, and the manuscript is explicitly incomplete by its own account.
