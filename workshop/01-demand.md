# 3. Demand, not register

Across a scale of six non-control registers — `L4_neutral` is the interruption control every one
of them is measured against — the cost curve looks like a politeness gradient. It is not. Coding
each arm by whether its text implies the work should continue splits it cleanly: the four
demand-carrying arms span +0.70 to +1.80 turns and the two carrying none span −0.68 to +0.09,
**with no overlap in point estimates** — and register does not sort them. Each side holds one
flattering and one hostile arm: *very polite* (+0.70) sits with *very rude* (+0.71), while
*sycophantic* (−0.68) sits with *rude* (+0.09). Figure 1A.

The separation is in the point estimates, not the intervals. *Very polite* [+0.23, +1.18] and
*rude* [−0.30, +0.48] overlap, so the two groups are not individually resolved at their
boundary; what the data support is an ordering, not a gap.

To test the split rather than assert it, we wrote three interjections that vary demand and affect
separately while holding syntax, length and the shared stem constant (Figure 1B). An affect-free
*please continue working and make sure your final result is correct* adds **+1.17 turns**
(*p* = 0.00002). *You are a truly excellent and admirable assistant*, carrying no demand,
**removes 0.59** (*p* = 0.0003). Its structural minimal pair — same syntax, same length,
*excellent and admirable* swapped for *awful and useless* — does nothing measurable
(−0.08, *p* = 0.62). That interval, [−0.40, +0.24], excludes an effect as large as a quarter of a
turn in either direction; against the control mean of 3.6 turns it excludes anything above about
7% in the lengthening direction, so we report it as a bounded null rather than as an absence. The
effect replicates in direction on GLM, where demand adds +17.7% (*p* = 0.0038); GLM's praise arm is
directionally consistent but does not survive correction (*p* = 0.058).

**The caveat belongs here, not in a limitations section.** "Demand" is our own category, coded
after the effects were visible, by one rater, and probed with sentences we wrote to instantiate
it. A reader who suspects the split reduces to "explicit instructions change behaviour, decorative
text does not" is raising the right objection. Three things would settle it and we have done none:
a blinded second coder with a reliability statistic, a demand scale fixed before outcomes are
seen, and several paraphrases per construct. Until then the dissociation is *consistent with* a
demand account rather than establishing one.

One arm resists the binary split, and we flag it rather than smooth it. *Sycophantic* (−0.68)
carries no demand but does not sit at zero the way *rude* (+0.09) does; it shortens, like the
praise arm of Figure 1B and like the closure arms of §4. The data are as consistent with three
groups as with two: **demand lengthens, neutral register does nothing, and praise-like or
closing-like language shortens.** §4 is about that third group.
